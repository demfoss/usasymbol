Faster answer than StateSymbolsUSA, clearer than Wikipedia, less bloated than generic animal or insect pages.

Official, verified, easy to read, and useful for students.

Section guidance:

Overview — title: "What Is the State Insect of [State]?"
Short and direct. Name the insect, give the official status, adoption year, and scientific name only if verified. Two to three sentences. Do not restate the intro word for word.

About — title: "About the [Insect Name]"
A short, simple section explaining what the insect looks like and why people recognize it. Three to five sentences max. Use visible details: color, markings, size, wings, sound, behavior, or season if verified. Do not turn this into a full biology profile.

Selection — title: "How It Became the State Insect"
Explain when it became official and how it was chosen. Mention students, teachers, organizations, lawmakers, or public campaigns only if verified. Keep it short. No bill numbers unless the YAML structure specifically requires them. Focus on the simple story, not legal language.

Reason — title: "Why [State] Chose the [Insect Name]"
Use this section only if a verified source explains why the insect was chosen.
If no source confirms the reason, do not invent symbolism. Say clearly that official sources name the insect but do not give a detailed reason, or skip this section if the YAML structure allows it.

Location — title: "Where You Can Find the [Insect Name]"
Keep this short. Say where the insect is found in the state only if verified. Avoid broad habitat claims unless supported by a reliable source. If state-specific location is not verified, describe only the general range or remove this detail.

Facts — title: "[Insect Name] Facts"
Three to five short verified facts.
Good facts include adoption year, scientific name, color, common markings, migration, school campaign, official source, or what makes the insect easy to recognize.
Do not add random trivia.

FAQ:
Short direct answers to real student questions. Use only questions that fit the available facts.

Style:
Write for a curious 12-year-old, not an entomology textbook.
Use active voice.
Keep sentences short.
Prefer concrete facts, dates, names, colors, and visible details.
Do not invent meaning, symbolism, or reasons.
If a source does not confirm why the insect was chosen, say that plainly or leave the reason out.
Avoid filler and generic nature writing.
