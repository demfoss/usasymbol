## Brief

QUERY

What is the Alabama state tree, when was it adopted, and why does the Southern longleaf pine matter so much to Alabama’s history

ANGLE

This page should center on the gap between what longleaf pine once was in Alabama—a dominant working tree that helped build the state—and what it is now: a reduced but still deeply symbolic species tied to restoration and identity.

FACTS

Alabama’s state tree is the Southern longleaf pine, also known as Pinus palustris.
Alabama adopted the state tree in 1949.
The 1949 bill named the southern pine as the official state tree.
Bill sponsor Hugh Kaul said he meant the longleaf pine.
In 1997, House Bill No. 533 specifically named the southern longleaf pine (Pinus palustris) as Alabama’s official state tree.
The longleaf pine was once abundant in Alabama.
The tree was important to Alabama’s economy through naval stores, lumber, and turpentine production.
Longleaf pine was used to build many historic structures in the state.
Today, longleaf pine is less common and has been outnumbered by loblolly pine.
Much of the area once occupied by longleaf pine has been replaced by loblolly and slash pine plantations.
Longleaf pine is associated with restoration efforts in Alabama.
Mature longleaf pine stands provide habitat for the federally endangered red-cockaded woodpecker.
Longleaf pine seedlings have a distinctive “grass stage” that helps protect the bud from fire.
The species is adapted to frequently burned environments.

    paragraphs:
      - "Alabama did not designate the longleaf pine as a gesture toward nature. In 1949, the choice was a recognition of economic history. The longleaf pine had been the foundation of Alabama's timber and naval stores industries for generations. Its resin was refined into turpentine. Its sap was processed into pitch and tar used to seal wooden ships — hence the term naval stores, a commodity Alabama produced in significant volume. Its straight-grained, resinous heartwood was cut into some of the most durable lumber available to nineteenth-century builders."
      - "Across the longleaf belt — a band of territory stretching through south and central Alabama into the coastal plain — sawmill towns rose and fell with the timber supply. The wood went into railroad ties, mill buildings, warehouses, and homes. Many structures built with old-growth longleaf pine are still standing because the wood is dense enough and resin-saturated enough to resist rot and insects in ways that younger, plantation-grown timber cannot match. When Alabama lawmakers in 1949 chose the southern pine as the state tree, they were acknowledging a commercial and industrial legacy that had defined the state's working economy for a century."
      - "Hugh Kaul, the bill's sponsor, later clarified that he had always meant the longleaf pine specifically — not the category of southern pines more broadly, which would include loblolly, shortleaf, and slash. But the 1949 law used the generic phrase 'southern pine,' and that vagueness would take nearly five decades to correct."

    icon: "fa-solid fa-landmark"
    title: "The 1949 Adoption and the 1997 Clarification"
    paragraphs:
      - "When Hugh Kaul sponsored the 1949 state tree bill, he was thinking of one tree. The law that passed named 'the southern pine' — a phrase broad enough to cover several species that grow in Alabama, including loblolly, which by then was already beginning to replace longleaf across much of its range. The ambiguity was probably not intentional. Kaul's stated intent was always the longleaf, and in 1949 most Alabamians working with timber or living in the wiregrass region would have understood 'the southern pine' as a reference to the tree that had once dominated the landscape."
      - "The problem was that the law did not say that. As loblolly pine plantations expanded across Alabama through the mid-twentieth century, the distinction between 'southern pine' and 'longleaf pine' became more than semantic. Loblolly grew faster, was easier to plantation-manage, and had effectively taken over much of the range once held by longleaf. A state tree designation that said 'southern pine' could be read as encompassing the tree that had replaced the one the original sponsor intended to honor."
      - "House Bill No. 533, passed in 1997, closed that gap. It named the Southern longleaf pine by both common name and scientific name — <em>Pinus palustris</em> — and made explicit what Kaul had always meant. The 1997 act did not change the 1949 adoption year, but it sharpened Alabama's commitment to a specific tree with a specific history."

  - id: "from-dominant-to-declining"
    icon: "fa-solid fa-chart-line"
    title: "From Alabama's Working Tree to a Reduced Forest Type"
    paragraphs:
      - "The longleaf pine once covered an estimated sixty to ninety million acres across the American South, with Alabama holding a substantial share of that range — particularly across the coastal plain, the wiregrass region of the southeast, and parts of central Alabama. It was not just a forest type. It was the landscape. Savannas of widely spaced longleaf pines over a carpet of wiregrass defined what much of south Alabama looked like before the industrial timber era."
      - "What replaced it happened fast by ecological standards. Industrial logging cleared old-growth longleaf stands from the late nineteenth century through the early twentieth. What grew back was often not replanted as longleaf. Loblolly pine — faster-growing, easier to manage in plantations, and more commercially efficient over short rotations — became the timber industry's preferred species across the South. Slash pine filled a similar role in wetter areas of the coastal plain. By the mid-twentieth century, the longleaf's range had contracted dramatically. Estimates suggest that fewer than three percent of the original longleaf pine ecosystem survives in anything approaching old-growth condition."
      - "In Alabama, the shift is visible in the timber data: loblolly pine now vastly outnumbers longleaf across the state's forested acreage. The tree that built Alabama's economy was quietly replaced by the tree that better fit industrial forestry's model. Alabama still has longleaf pine — in the Conecuh National Forest, on state and private lands managed for it specifically — but it occupies a fraction of its former territory."

    icon: "fa-solid fa-seedling"
    title: "What the Longleaf Pine Represents Now"
    paragraphs:
      - "Longleaf pine is a strange kind of state symbol — one that is most meaningful precisely because it is less common than it once was. The designation does not simply commemorate what the tree was. It points toward what people are working to bring back."
      - "The tree itself is built for a long game. Longleaf pine seedlings spend their first years in what ecologists call the grass stage — a phase in which the above-ground growth looks like a clump of long grass while the seedling puts its energy into developing a deep root system and a protected bud. The bud sits low enough and is shielded well enough that fire, which once swept regularly through longleaf savannas, cannot kill it. This adaptation is the key to the longleaf's ecology: it is not just fire-tolerant but fire-dependent, requiring periodic burns to suppress competing vegetation and maintain the open savanna conditions it evolved in. Suppress the fire long enough and longleaf gives way to hardwoods and other species that cannot survive in a burning landscape."
      - "The red-cockaded woodpecker, listed as federally endangered, depends on mature longleaf pine stands almost exclusively. The bird excavates nest cavities in living longleaf pines — specifically old trees with the fungal heart rot that makes excavation possible — and its population decline has tracked closely with the loss of longleaf forest. In Alabama, conservation of the red-cockaded woodpecker and restoration of longleaf pine are effectively the same project."
      - "Across Alabama, land managers on public and private land have been replanting longleaf and reintroducing prescribed fire to rebuild something like the pine savannas that once defined the region. It is slow work — longleaf grows slowly, and the grass stage alone can last several years. But the effort reflects a serious reckoning with what was lost when industrial forestry replaced the forest that had been there for thousands of years."
      - "The longleaf pine still builds things in Alabama. It built the economy. Now it is helping rebuild the habitat, the watershed function, and the ecological structure of a landscape that had been simplified almost past recognition. As a state symbol, it earns its place not through sentiment but through that specific, documented, ongoing Alabama story. It sits alongside the [Alabama state flower](/states/alabama/flower) and the [Alabama state bird](/states/alabama/bird) as one of the symbols that most directly ties official designation to the state's environmental history."

It is slow work because longleaf grows slowly, but it represents a sustained effort to recover something close to the pine savannas that once defined much of south Alabama."

The tree defined the working landscape of south and central Alabama before industrial forestry replaced most of its range with faster-growing species."

  - question: "Why is the longleaf pine less common now"
    answer: "Industrial logging cleared most old-growth longleaf in Alabama by the early twentieth century. The cleared land was often replanted with loblolly or slash pine, which grow faster and suit commercial timber production better. Fire suppression also worked against longleaf, which depends on periodic burns to maintain the open savanna conditions it evolved in. Today loblolly pine vastly outnumbers longleaf across Alabama's forested land."

  - question: "What is the grass stage of longleaf pine"
    answer: "Longleaf pine seedlings spend their first years in a grass stage — a period in which above-ground growth resembles a clump of long grass while the seedling develops a deep root system and a protected bud. The low, shielded bud allows the seedling to survive the periodic fires that longleaf savannas historically experienced. This adaptation is central to the species' ecology and one reason prescribed fire is essential to longleaf restoration."

  - question: "What wildlife depends on longleaf pine in Alabama"
    answer: "The red-cockaded woodpecker — listed as federally endangered — depends almost exclusively on mature longleaf pine stands for nesting. The bird excavates cavities in living longleaf pines, specifically older trees with softened heartwood caused by fungal rot. Its population decline has closely tracked the loss of old-growth longleaf forest. In Alabama, conserving the woodpecker and restoring longleaf pine are effectively the same effort."

  - question: "Is there longleaf pine restoration happening in Alabama"
    answer: "Yes. Restoration work is ongoing across Alabama on public lands — including the Conecuh National Forest — and on private land managed for longleaf. Prescribed fire has been reintroduced as a management tool to rebuild savanna habitat. Longleaf grows slowly, and the grass stage alone can last several years, but the effort represents a sustained attempt to recover something close to the pine savannas that once defined much of south Alabama."

  - question: "Who sponsored the original Alabama state tree legislation"
    answer: "Hugh Kaul sponsored the 1949 bill that designated the southern pine as Alabama's official state tree. He later stated he had always meant the longleaf pine specifically."
